Dark Matter

The universe is so expansive that society only knows roughly about 5% of it. With the universe being so massive and unknown, how can society develop from here on about what we know about it? Truth is that we already have expanded our knowledge of the universe as we know it throughout the ages with the help of studying the unknown. Dark matter was officially coined as a term in the 1970s after years of speculation about its existence, being dubbed the “glue of the universe” by Vera Rubin (Primack). Rubin proposed this theory of dark matter's existence, which has led to many advancements in technology throughout the years trying to study it. Not only study it, but prove its existence, as how do you study something that is invisible to the natural eye. Trying to figure out the answer, science has created many byproducts of trying to study invisible matter. Many advancements in technology have been made either as a byproduct of studying dark matter or as a direct method. Some of these advancements include the development of hypersensitive sensors such as the DAMIC-M, advancements in cryogenic technology, and supercomputers such as the AURORA (Bautista). With the development of newer technologies, not only has the computing and physics field of science been affected, but those such as medicine (with the further development of cryogenics) have also propelled further. With the study of dark matter being in question of why society should care about continuing to fund the research into it, they must reflect and see what has come from researching it since it was first discovered in the 1930s.

A Brief Look Into History

The first known detection of dark matter was in the 1930s by a Swiss-American astronomer by the name of Fritz Zwicky. Zwicky was studying the mass of stars in the Coma cluster where he first described something as “missing mass”, a phenomenon that couldn't be explained at the time (Riess). Now of course society describes this as dark matter, but at the time it was unknown what was keeping the planets from flying away from another. Many scientists attribute it to gravity, that the universe worked like a halo or a disk where gravity was always flattening them causing the particles to never move too far apart. However, it wasn't until the 1970s when American astronomers Vera Rubin and W. Kent Ford confirmed the existence of the “missing matter” instead dubbing it a dark matter due to the fact that it was invisible. With the reason of dark matter being founded as an idea due as stated by Britcanna, that the mass of stars visible only make up about 10% of the gravity required to keep it from flying away from the centre. Meaning that something else is keeping the star's orbital velocity neutral so that it does not separate (Riess). This was when scientists truly started to look into the possibility of there being invisible matter in the universe. Something that couldn't be seen, but was acting like a glue keeping the natural balance of gravity and velocity of a universe in check. It was not too long after where gravitational lensing (the act of light bending due to the gravitational field of a large object) was seen in an area of no visible matter, meaning something was there.
